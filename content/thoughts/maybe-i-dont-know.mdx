Something strange has happened to people.

Everyone knows everything now.

Ask a hard question at work and nobody answers anymore. They present. Three bullet points, a diagram nobody asked for, and a roadmap. Phase 1: quick wins. Phase 2: scale. Phase 3 is never explained, because by then everyone has forgotten what the original question was.

Behind the roadmap there is usually an agent now, or several. One agent drafts it. Another agent checks whether it sounds confident enough. A third agent manages the first two, and somewhere above all of them sits a manager agent whose entire job is making sure the other agents are managing each other correctly. Nobody is quite sure what happens if you ask the manager agent a direct question, and nobody has tried, because that feels like the kind of thing that ends careers.

Nobody says "I don't know." That sentence has been deprecated. It's been replaced by something far more useful: a statistic nobody can trace.

> "Studies show 73% of people are happier when they journal."
> 
> "Which study?"
> 
> Silence. Then, quietly, "I think Claude said it."
> 
> "Where did Claude get it?"
> 
> "Some website, I think. It had a photo of a woman looking very pleased about a spreadsheet."

We have officially turned "I found this on the internet" into a research methodology. Peer review is now just asking a second chatbot if the first one sounded confident.

Somewhere along the way, confidence itself became the benchmark. A chatbot never says "I'm not sure," it just says the thing, cleanly, in full sentences, with no hedging. And somehow that became the standard we now hold each other to as well. Say it like you know. Sound certain, structured, resolved. Nobody is grading you on whether you're right anymore, just on whether you sounded like you were.

Even the small stuff has become suspiciously well written. Birthday messages now read like they were drafted by the same unusually enthusiastic HR manager. Condolences have gotten eloquent in a way that should probably concern us.

> "Your loss is immeasurable. May the memories you shared bring comfort during this difficult time."

Beautifully put. Also, statistically unlikely that the person sending it has ever said "immeasurable" out loud in casual conversation, or possibly ever.

Maybe that's what actually bothers me. Not that the words are bad, but that they're too good. Someone fumbling to say "I don't know what to say, I'm just here" is more human than three paragraphs about healing and cherished memories that could be sent to literally anyone with minor find and replace.

> I don't know.  
> I might be wrong.  
> There isn't enough information.  
> I don't know what to say.

Nobody wants to say these anymore, because they sound like weakness instead of what they actually are, which is someone being honest about the limits of what they know. So instead we reach for a framework, or a stat with no source, or a sentence a machine already polished for us, because all three sound more certain than the truth usually does.

I use AI constantly, for what it's worth. Not to skip the thinking, mostly to get the boring parts of a day out of the way faster, or occasionally to argue with something that won't get tired of arguing back.

But I keep coming back to the same thought. There's a difference between having an answer and having understood the question. Between sounding certain and being uncertain for the right reasons.

Maybe the most intelligent thing a person can say still isn't "here are five possible solutions."

Maybe it's "I don't know."

And then, for once, actually thinking about it.
