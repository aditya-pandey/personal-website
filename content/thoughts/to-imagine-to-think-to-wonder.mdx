I have a habit I've never really been able to describe. Whenever I become curious about something, my mind doesn't stop at the explanation. It starts walking backwards.

Let's say I read about the invention of the wheel. Most people would read about when it was invented, where it first appeared, how it changed trade and eventually civilization. I read those things too, but then another question quietly appears: What did the day before the wheel look like?

What happened a year before? The century before or Just the ordinary day before someone, somewhere, stumbled upon an idea that would outlive empires.  
That's usually when the little movie begins.

For just the sheer joy of this mental time travel, I place myself inside that world. Not as a participant, but as an invisible observer. Nobody knows I'm there. I can't influence anything. I'm just watching. My brain even insists on giving me a ridiculously modern orange sofa to sit on in the middle of a prehistoric forest. It has become a recurring prop at this point. I have no explanation for it, and I've stopped trying to find one.

Then the scene slowly comes alive.

There isn't a narrator announcing that humanity is about to invent the wheel. There are simply people living another ordinary day. Someone is dragging something heavy across the ground and is clearly annoyed. Another person notices that a fallen log rolls more easily than it slides. Someone tries something. It doesn't quite work. Someone else laughs. A child notices something nobody else does. A few days later they try again.

I know none of this actually happened exactly this way.  
But something like it must have.

History has a habit of compressing thousands of ordinary moments into one extraordinary sentence. "The wheel was invented." "Humans discovered fire." "Language evolved." Those sentences are true, but they also erase almost everything interesting. They hide the failed attempts, the accidents, the tiny observations that slowly accumulated into something we now call an invention.  
That's the part my imagination refuses to skip.

Language is probably my favorite example. I often find myself wondering what the first intentional word looked like. Not linguistically. Humanly.

Imagine a small group of early humans who have never thought of language as something they are creating, because of course they aren't trying to create it. They're simply trying to communicate. Maybe someone points toward water and makes a particular sound. Maybe someone else repeats it the next day. Maybe the sound slowly stops being random and starts meaning the same thing every time. At what point does a repeated noise quietly become a word? There isn't a ceremony for that. Nobody celebrates it. Nobody even realizes it's happening.

Centuries later we simply say that language evolved.

My mind wants to watch the centuries we skipped.  
The same thing happens whenever I think about religion. I don't immediately think about scriptures or civilizations or institutions. I imagine a tiny group of people sitting around a fire after something they can't explain. Maybe someone survived a storm they shouldn't have survived. Maybe somebody dreamt about a loved one who had died. Maybe the moon disappeared one night and returned a few days later. People begin trying to explain things with the limited understanding they have. One person offers an idea. Someone else disagrees. Another person builds upon it. Nobody in that conversation has the slightest idea that they might be participating in the beginning of something that will shape billions of lives.  
That thought fascinates me.

What I keep coming back to is the transition. The awkward, unnamed period where nothing has settled yet and nobody knows they are making history. We tend to imagine history as a series of important events. But I suspect it felt painfully ordinary while people were actually living it.

Nobody woke up thinking they were about to change the world. They were probably just trying to solve whatever problem yesterday had left behind.  
Every certainty we live with today was once somebody's unfinished thought. That idea has stayed with me longer than most things I've read or been taught. It makes history feel less like a record and more like a conversation that never really ended.  
History, when you zoom out, looks inevitable.

History, when you zoom in, looks like ordinary people making imperfect decisions without knowing where those decisions will lead.

And for some reason, that's where I always end up.

Not in the pages of the history book, but in the spaces between its sentences, sitting on an orange sofa that has absolutely no business existing there, quietly watching humanity figure itself out one tiny idea at a time.
