*On Flatland, the limits of imagination, and learning to see what we cannot.*

<ExploreToggle />

Imagine living in a world with no height.

Not a world where everything is short. A world where height simply doesn't exist.

There is length and breadth, but nothing above or below. No cubes, no spheres, no trees. Everything that exists must fit within these two dimensions.

Now imagine that you live there.

You can move around, recognise shapes, build things, and develop a perfectly reasonable understanding of your world. You might even become a very intelligent person.

But there is one thing you cannot do.

You cannot imagine height.

What would you make of someone trying to explain a direction you had never experienced? Where would you put it? How would you even begin to picture it?

This is the idea behind *Flatland*, a novella published by Edwin Abbott Abbott in 1884. Its inhabitants are geometric shapes living in a two-dimensional world. When a Square encounters a being from the third dimension, his understanding of reality is challenged.

It is a story about geometry, but it raises a much bigger question: how much of what we consider impossible is actually beyond reality, and how much is simply beyond our imagination?

Let's start small. With a point. I am, after all, trying to make one.

---

## 1. From a point to a cube

Consider a point.

It has no length, no width, no height. Just a position.

<PointToLine>A point has a position and nothing else. Moved in one direction, it traces a line, which has exactly one dimension: length. One number is enough to say where you are on it.</PointToLine>

When the point moves in one direction, it traces a line.

Now let that line move sideways, in a direction perpendicular to its length. As it moves, it traces a square.

<LineToSquare>The line moves at right angles to itself, by exactly its own length. Its two ends trace two new edges; the original line and its moved copy are the other two. There are now two independent directions: length and breadth.</LineToSquare>

The square has something the line didn't: a second independent direction. We can move horizontally and vertically across its surface.

Now imagine moving the entire square away from its original plane, in a new direction perpendicular to it.

A cube.

<SquareToCube>First the square turns, so we can see the plane it lies in. Then it moves out of that plane, at right angles to it, by the length of its own side. Each corner traces an edge, and the result is a cube. Dashed lines are edges hidden behind the faces in front.</SquareToCube>

The pattern is surprisingly simple.

A point moves to create a line. A line moves to create a square. A square moves to create a cube.

Each time, we add a new direction.

Of course, the drawings on your screen are imperfect representations. A mathematical point has no physical size, and a line drawn on a screen has some thickness. But the idea is what matters: we can use movement in a new direction to understand the next dimension.

Now comes the interesting part.

What happens when we try to do this again?

---

## 2. Try being a Flatlander

Imagine that you are a Square living on a flat surface. You know left and right, forward and backward. Everything you have ever seen exists within your two-dimensional world.

One day, a sphere passes through your plane.

What do you see?

<SphereThroughPlane>One view is from outside: Flatland edge-on, as a line, with a sphere passing down through it. The other shows the plane itself from above, and the only part of the sphere that is ever in it: where the two meet. For a sphere of radius 1 whose centre is a height h from the plane, that meeting is a circle of radius √(1 − h²). It begins as a point, is largest as the centre crosses, shrinks to a point again, and then there is nothing. A Flatlander, looking along the plane, would see even less: only the edge of that circle, a line that grows and shrinks. The strip along the bottom keeps a record of what appeared.</SphereThroughPlane>

First, a point appears. Then a line, which you would eventually work out is the edge of a circle. The circle grows larger, reaches its maximum size, and begins to shrink. Eventually, it disappears.

You never see the sphere itself. You only see the part that intersects your world.

Imagine trying to explain the event to another Flatlander. You might argue that a strange circle appeared from nowhere and vanished. You might spend years trying to understand what happened.

And you would be working with incomplete information, despite reasoning perfectly well from what you could observe.

This is what makes *Flatland* such an interesting thought experiment. You don't need to lack intelligence to misunderstand something. You might simply lack access to the whole picture.

Now turn the question around.

What would it mean for us to be the Square?

---

## 3. What comes after a cube?

We live in three spatial dimensions. We can move left and right, forward and backward, up and down.

In mathematics, we can describe a point in this space using three coordinates: *x*, *y*, and *z*.

But suppose there were another spatial direction, independent of all three.

Not north or east, because those are already directions within our familiar space. Imagine a genuinely new direction, perpendicular to all three.

This is not time. Physics does treat time as a dimension, but it is not a direction you could slide a cube along.

What would happen if we moved a cube along it?

Following the same pattern, we would get a four-dimensional analogue of a cube: a **tesseract**, also called a four-dimensional hypercube.

<Tesseract>The same construction once more. A cube is copied and moved along a fourth direction, w. Nothing on a page or a screen can point that way, so the drawing uses a common convention: the copy is drawn larger, as if it had moved outward, and matching corners are joined. What you see is a projection, a three-dimensional shadow of a four-dimensional object, flattened again onto your screen. As it turns through w, the cubes seem to pass through each other and swap places. That distortion belongs to the shadow, not to the tesseract.</Tesseract>

The construction follows the same logic as before. The problem is that we cannot point towards this fourth spatial direction the way we can point upwards or sideways.

So how do we draw it?

We project it into three dimensions, much like we project a three-dimensional object onto a flat sheet of paper. The familiar image of one cube inside another, with their corners connected, is one way of representing a tesseract.

It isn't the tesseract itself. It's a representation that helps us reason about its structure.

And that distinction matters.

We can describe a mathematical object without being able to visualise it in its entirety. A tesseract has 16 vertices, 32 edges, 24 square faces, and 8 cubic cells. We can establish these properties mathematically, even though our drawings are only projections.

We can know more than we can picture.

---

## 4. Do we have to imagine everything to understand it?

Try to imagine a tesseract for a moment.

Perhaps you see a cube inside another cube. Perhaps you picture a rotating wireframe. Maybe the whole thing becomes a confusing collection of lines.

Whatever you're imagining, you're probably working with a three-dimensional representation of a four-dimensional structure.

And that's perfectly fine.

There is a difference between being able to picture something and being able to understand it. We can reason about mathematical relationships without turning them into images in our heads.

But there's another distinction worth making: understanding a mathematical object doesn't establish that it exists as a physical object in our universe. Higher spatial dimensions appear in some physical theories, but whether they describe physical reality is a separate question requiring evidence.

<WaysOfKnowing>One structure, held four ways. A description in symbols and a table of counts let us reason about it. A drawing lets us picture part of it. None of them settles the fourth question, whether such a thing exists in the physical world. That calls for evidence.</WaysOfKnowing>

Our imagination has limits. That doesn't automatically make everything beyond those limits real.

It does, however, give us a reason to be careful about dismissing ideas simply because they are difficult to picture.

The Square has no intuitive experience of height. Yet we can explain the logic of a third dimension to him through constructions, analogies, and arguments.

He doesn't have to see it the way we do to start understanding it.

Perhaps we don't either.

---

## 5. The limits of our world

What I find most interesting about *Flatland* is how ordinary the Square's limitations seem from the inside.

His world makes sense to him. He has ways of explaining what he observes. He has no particular reason to suspect that an entire direction is missing.

Until he encounters something he cannot explain.

We do something similar whenever we mistake what feels intuitive for what must be true. Our experience shapes the way we understand the world, and sometimes we forget that experience is only a starting point.

But the lesson isn't that everything we cannot imagine must exist. It is that our imagination alone cannot settle the question.

Maybe expanding our imagination means becoming more willing to examine our assumptions. To explore an unfamiliar idea before dismissing it. To accept that we might be missing something, without filling the gap with whatever explanation happens to sound exciting.

It means being comfortable with the possibility that we don't yet understand.

We began with a point, moved it into a line, moved the line into a square, and moved the square into a cube. Then we used the same reasoning to explore an object we cannot easily visualise as a whole.

No magic was involved. Just a pattern, a question, and the willingness to follow an idea somewhere unfamiliar.

And perhaps that's the value of the exercise.

**What if the limits of your imagination are not the limits of what you can understand?**

Not everything beyond our understanding is real. But neither does something become impossible just because we struggle to picture it.

Sometimes, the first step towards understanding another dimension is learning that seeing isn't the only way to know.

### A note on the science

- The fourth dimension in this essay is a direction in space, not time. Relativity does treat spacetime as four-dimensional, but time behaves differently from the three directions of space.
- Extra spatial dimensions are hypothetical. They appear in some theories, string theory among them, but no experiment has found one. Gravity behaves as three dimensions predict down to about 50 micrometres, and gravitational waves from a neutron star collision in 2017 behaved as expected for three dimensions of space plus time.
- Every tesseract on this page is a projection. Its distortions, including the way it seems to turn inside out, belong to the projection, not to the object.
- A Flatlander could never see a circle, only line segments of changing length. The view from above in Fig. 4 is ours, not theirs.
- Flatland is a thought experiment. Physicists have argued since Paul Ehrenfest in 1917 that stable orbits and atoms need three dimensions of space, so a real two-dimensional world might not work at all.
- Popular explanations of higher dimensions sometimes picture curved space bending into an extra direction. In general relativity, curvature is intrinsic: space can be curved without anything to curve into. Measurements so far also find the space of our universe very close to flat.
- Some studies suggest people can build a limited intuition for four dimensions with practice in virtual reality. It is nothing like the ease with which we picture a cube.
